Notes on reading surfaces

Most of what we read now arrives in a rectangle of light. The rectangle changes size from one moment to the next: a phone held in one hand on a train, a laptop balanced on a kitchen table, a large monitor at work. Text has to survive all of these, and the people reading it bring their own eyes, habits, and patience to each one. Most of what we read now arrives in a rectangle of light. Typographers have argued about line length for longer than screens have existed. A line that is too short forces the eye back to the left margin so often that the rhythm of a sentence breaks apart. A line that is too long makes the return journey difficult, and readers sometimes land on the wrong line and have to search for their place. Line spacing has a similar story. Extra space between lines can make each line easier to pick out, but it also spreads a paragraph over more of the screen, which means more scrolling and fewer lines visible at once. Whether that trade is worth making depends on the text, the reader, and the size of the type. Line spacing has a similar story. Size matters too, though not in the way a number in a style sheet suggests. Two fonts set at the same nominal size can look quite different, because one has tall lowercase letters and the other short ones. What the reader actually sees depends on the shape of the letters, the distance to the screen, and how well the display draws thin strokes.
